Basic Surgical Principles

20 quiz items per set

  • TOS emphasis: 20 items / 20% of Surgery, with 10/20 items at Applying level; prioritize the immediate operative or resuscitative decision.

  • Board rule: when physiology and imaging conflict in an unstable surgical patient, physiology usually determines whether to operate now.

1. Fluid–Electrolyte Disorders

High-yield element Rapid review
Named signs Chvostek sign and Trousseau sign may occur with hypomagnesemia/hypocalcemia; refractory hypokalemia or hypocalcemia should prompt magnesium correction.
Key cutoff → decision Symptomatic hyponatremia, often at Na ≤120 mEq/L → controlled 3% saline; avoid correction >1 mEq/L/h or >12 mEq/L/day. Chronic hypernatremia → correct even more slowly.
Study of choice Serum electrolytes + osmolality + renal function; ECG is critical with potassium abnormalities.
Operate now Only if the surgical disease itself is emergent; correct life-threatening electrolyte disturbance concurrently.
Observe/optimize first Elective surgery with severe symptomatic Na/K/Ca/Mg derangement.
Procedure of choice No primary operation; treatment is physiologic correction.
Classic structure at risk If central venous access is required: carotid artery/pleural dome depending on route.
Top complication Overrapid hyponatremia correction → osmotic demyelination; timing is neurologic rather than a fixed POD.
Contraindicated/wrong maneuver Do not correct chronic sodium disorders rapidly; do not treat refractory K solely with more K when Mg is low.

2. Acid–Base Balance

High-yield element Rapid review
Named sign Kussmaul respirations → compensatory hyperventilation in metabolic acidosis.
Scoring/cutoff Winter formula: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8. Anion gap: Na − (Cl + HCO₃⁻); Schwartz uses normal <≈12 mmol/L.
Shock severity marker Base deficit −3 to −5 mild; −6 to −9 moderate; ≤−10 severe.
Operate now Lactic acidosis from uncontrolled hemorrhage or surgically correctable septic source → control the source.
Damage control Trauma with pH <7.2, temperature <35°C, marked base deficit/coagulopathy → abbreviate surgery.
Definitive repair After restoration of temperature, perfusion, coagulation, and acid-base status.
Top complication Persistent acidosis → myocardial depression/coagulopathy/MODS; no fixed POD.
Contraindicated/wrong maneuver Do not treat hemorrhagic lactic acidosis with bicarbonate instead of restoring perfusion. Schwartz emphasizes volume/perfusion correction.

3. Endocrine and Metabolic Response to Injury

High-yield element Rapid review
Core pattern ↑ Catecholamines/cortisol/glucagon → insulin resistance, lipolysis, gluconeogenesis, proteolysis, negative nitrogen balance.
Scoring system No source-defined operative score.
Diagnostic focus Glucose, electrolytes, phosphate, Mg, weight/intake trends, organ function.
Decision point Life-saving surgery should not be delayed merely to normalize stress hyperglycemia.
Observe/optimize Elective surgery with major uncontrolled endocrine disease when correction is feasible.
Nutrition cutoff Schwartz gives 25–30 kcal/kg/day as a practical initial caloric estimate for many critically ill adults.
Top complication Catabolism → muscle loss, infection risk, delayed wound healing; no fixed POD.
Contraindicated/wrong maneuver Avoid aggressive overfeeding as a strategy to “reverse” the stress response.

4. Shock and Critical Care

Hemorrhagic shock classification

Class Blood loss HR BP CNS Decision
I <750 mL / <15% <100 Normal Normal Observe response; crystalloid/blood as clinically needed
II 750–1500 mL / 15–30% >100 Orthostatic Anxious Escalate resuscitation
III 1500–2000 mL / 30–40% >120 Hypotension Confused Blood products + rapid source control
IV >2000 mL / >40% >140 Severe hypotension Obtunded Massive transfusion + immediate hemorrhage control

  • Named/significant clue: tachycardia is not sufficiently sensitive to exclude hemorrhage; normal HR does not reassure.

  • Imaging of choice: unstable abdomen → FAST; stable/stabilized high-energy trauma → contrast CT. CT must not delay source control in persistent shock.

  • Operate now: nonresponder/transient responder with suspected surgically controllable hemorrhage.

  • Observe/NOM: hemodynamically stable patient with an injury amenable to close observation ± embolization.

  • Procedure of choice: source-dependent laparotomy/thoracotomy/vascular intervention; in exsanguinating abdominal trauma, hemorrhage control precedes reconstruction.

  • Top complication: MODS after prolonged shock; no single fixed POD.

  • Contraindicated maneuver: CT transport for a persistently unstable bleeding patient.

5. Trauma

ATLS

  • A — Airway + C-spine.

  • B — Breathing.

  • C — Circulation + hemorrhage control.

  • D — Disability/GCS.

  • E — Exposure + prevent hypothermia.

Glasgow Coma Scale

Component Maximum
Eyes 4
Verbal 5
Motor 6
Total 3–15

  • Schwartz confirms GCS 3–15 during early trauma assessment.

  • [SUPPLEMENTARY] GCS ≤8 is a classic threshold for considering definitive airway protection.

Decision rules

  • Operate now: shock + peritonitis; uncontrolled hemorrhage; unstable abdominal patient with positive FAST.

  • Observe: stable solid-organ injury meeting nonoperative criteria with monitoring and immediate access to intervention.

  • Failure of NOM: new hypotension, rising transfusion requirement, peritonitis, worsening lactate/base deficit.

  • Damage control: hypothermia <35°C + pH <7.2 + severe coagulopathy/base deficit → packing/contamination control/temporary closure.

  • Definitive repair: after physiologic normalization in ICU.

  • Classic structure at risk: Pringle maneuver encircles portal triad; careless application risks common bile duct/portal structures. Schwartz describes portal vein, hepatic artery, and common bile duct within the pedicle.

  • Contraindicated: prolonged definitive reconstruction in a patient spiraling into hypothermia–acidosis–coagulopathy.

Burn decision points

  • Named assessment: Rule of Nines estimates adult TBSA.

  • Classic presentation: full-thickness burn = leathery, nonblanching, relatively painless; partial thickness = blistering, wet, painful.

  • Parkland/Baxter: Schwartz describes 3–4 mL × kg × %TBSA LR, half in first 8 h from the time of burn, remainder over 16 h.

  • Schwartz also notes a more recent ABA starting formula of 2 mL/kg/%TBSA because of excessive fluid administration with older estimates.

  • Resuscitation endpoints: adult urine output ≈30 mL/h and MAP ≈60 mmHg in the source chapter.

  • Operate now: circumferential burn with vascular compromise → escharotomy; deep compartment syndrome → fasciotomy.

  • Observe: superficial and many superficial partial-thickness burns.

  • Procedure of choice: deep partial-thickness/full-thickness wounds unlikely to heal → excision + skin grafting.

  • Airway: hoarseness, stridor, dyspnea, progressive airway edema → early endotracheal intubation.

  • Top complication: overresuscitation → compartment syndromes/pleural effusions, usually during early resuscitation.

  • Contraindicated: wait for obvious airway obstruction before intubating a progressively edematous burn airway; formula-driven fluid without endpoint reassessment.

7. Wound Healing

  • Phases: hemostasis/inflammation → proliferation → maturation/remodeling.

  • Named closure categories: primary, secondary, and tertiary/delayed primary intention.

  • Strength: Schwartz describes maximal wound strength around 6 weeks, ultimately about 75–80% of uninjured tissue.

  • Operate now: fascial dehiscence/evisceration → urgent operative reclosure.

  • Observe: superficial skin separation with intact fascia and no deep infection.

  • Procedure: debridement of necrotic tissue; delayed closure when contamination requires initial open management.

  • Classic structure at risk: fascia—failure produces evisceration/incisional hernia.

  • Top complication: fascial dehiscence; [SUPPLEMENTARY] classic board timing POD 5–8.

  • Contraindicated: primary closure over uncontrolled infection or devitalized tissue.

8. Surgical Infection

Wound classification

Class Definition/example Decision relevance
I Clean No infected field/no hollow viscus entered Lowest SSI risk
II Clean-contaminated Controlled entry into respiratory/GI/GU tract Prophylaxis indicated
III Contaminated Major contamination/traumatic wound/spillage Higher SSI risk; treatment strategy escalates
IV Dirty Established infection/perforated viscus/necrotizing infection Treat as established infection, not prophylaxis

  • Named sign: no single sensitive named maneuver; look for purulence, fluctuance, crepitus, disproportionate pain.

  • Imaging of choice: CT for occult deep/organ-space collection in a stable patient.

  • Operate now: necrotizing soft-tissue infection, uncontrolled perforation, drainable abscess requiring surgical drainage.

  • Observe/medical management: cellulitis without collection; selected contained infection suitable for antibiotics ± image-guided drainage.

  • Procedure of choice: incision/drainage for abscess; aggressive debridement for necrotizing infection.

  • Top complication: SSI commonly becomes evident around POD 7–10 in Schwartz.

  • Contraindicated: waiting for CT in an unstable patient with obvious necrotizing infection; treating an abscess with antibiotics alone.

9. Asepsis / Antisepsis

  • Key classification: use Class I–IV wound contamination above.

  • Antibiotic timing: adequate drug concentration must be present at incision; late administration after contamination is less effective.

  • Redose: lengthy cases exceeding antibiotic half-life or with major blood loss/fluid replacement.

  • Operate now: no primary operation unless contamination has produced a drainable/invasive infection.

  • Observe: uncomplicated clean wound with standard prophylaxis.

  • Classic structure at risk: implanted mesh/valve/prosthesis because contamination may establish biofilm.

  • Top complication: SSI; usually several days after operation rather than immediate.

  • Contraindicated: prolonged “prophylaxis” as a substitute for drainage/source control.

10. Surgical Nutrition

  • Named syndrome: Refeeding syndrome → severe hypophosphatemia, potentially respiratory failure.

  • Energy target: 25–30 kcal/kg/day is a practical initial estimate in many critically ill patients.

  • Route of choice: enteral nutrition if the gut is functional.

  • TPN: reserve for inaccessible/nonfunctional GI tract or inability to meet requirements enterally.

  • Imaging: none routinely; feeding-tube position requires appropriate confirmation according to route.

  • Operate: feeding gastrostomy/jejunostomy when prolonged enteral access is required and anatomy/clinical context justify it.

  • Classic structure at risk: central venous access → pleura/carotid depending on site; jejunostomy → mesenteric blood supply.

  • Top complication: TPN catheter sepsis or electrolyte disturbance; no fixed POD.

  • Contraindicated: aggressive full-calorie feeding immediately in a severely malnourished patient at refeeding risk.

11. Blood Transfusion / Hemostasis

  • Classic distinction: focal operative bleeding → suspect mechanical source; diffuse oozing → consider coagulopathy.

  • Massive-trauma strategy: Schwartz discusses balanced 1:1:1 plasma:platelet:RBC resuscitation.

  • Platelet target: Schwartz cites keeping platelets above about 50 × 10⁹/L in an actively bleeding patient.

  • Study of choice: CBC, PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen ± TEG/ROTEM.

  • Operate now: ongoing surgically controllable hemorrhage despite resuscitation.

  • Procedure of choice: direct hemorrhage control + massive transfusion protocol, not transfusion alone.

  • Classic structure at risk: bleeding vessel itself; source control outranks laboratory normalization.

  • Top complication: acute transfusion reaction → during/immediately after transfusion; massive transfusion also causes early hypocalcemia, hypothermia, coagulopathy.

  • Delayed complication: Schwartz describes post-transfusion purpura around day 5–6.

  • Contraindicated: continue a transfusion when an acute hemolytic reaction is suspected.

12. Oncology

  • Scoring/staging: TNM = Tumor, Nodes, Metastasis; exact cutoffs are organ-specific and are not universal.

  • Imaging of choice: tumor-specific staging CT/MRI/PET as appropriate; obtain distant staging before major surgery when metastatic risk could alter intent.

  • Absolute operative principle: resectable malignancy for which surgery offers cure/control and an R0/negative-margin resection is achievable.

  • Procedure of choice: en bloc tumor resection with appropriate margin ± regional lymph-node management.

  • Observe/systemic treatment first: unresectable/metastatic disease unless surgery is needed for bleeding, obstruction, infection, perforation, or palliation.

  • Classic structure at risk: organ-specific; regional nerves/vessels and lymphatic basin.

  • Top complication: site-specific; no universal POD.

  • Contraindicated: tumor violation/fragmentation or sacrificing oncologic margins merely to perform a smaller procedure.

  • Board distractor: more radical surgery is not automatically better if adequate negative margins can be obtained with a less morbid operation.

13. Immunology

  • Key distinction: postoperative inflammation ≠ infection; immunosuppressed patients may show muted signs.

  • No source-defined surgical score determines operation.

  • Imaging of choice: source-directed imaging for suspected infection.

  • Operate now: abscess, necrosis, perforation, graft-threatening technical complication—immunosuppression does not remove the need for source control.

  • Observe/medical management: immune dysregulation without surgical source.

  • Classic risk: excessive immunosuppression → infection/malignancy; inadequate immunosuppression → rejection.

  • Top complication: infection; timing varies.

  • Contraindicated: reflexively increase immunosuppression for graft dysfunction before excluding infection/technical causes.

14. Transplantation

Hyperacute Immediate/very early Preformed antibody–mediated graft failure Urgent graft assessment; often irreversible
Acute Early to later Cellular and/or antibody mediated Confirm, often with biopsy; intensify directed immunotherapy
Chronic Months–years Progressive vascular/fibrotic loss Long-term graft-management strategy

  • Imaging of choice: graft-specific Doppler/vascular imaging when acute dysfunction raises concern for thrombosis or inflow/outflow failure.

  • Operate now: vascular thrombosis, uncontrolled bleeding, leak, torsion/technical catastrophe threatening graft viability.

  • Observe/treat medically: stable graft dysfunction due to reversible medication/immunologic issue once technical complications are excluded.

  • Procedure of choice: urgent graft salvage/revision for technical vascular complication.

  • Classic structure at risk: [SUPPLEMENTARY kidney transplant] iliac vessels and ureteral blood supply.

  • Top complication: graft thrombosis is early; rejection timing varies by type.

  • Contraindicated: diagnose every abrupt graft dysfunction as rejection without first excluding vascular obstruction/technical failure.

15. Anesthesia

ASA Physical Status

ASA Definition Decision relevance
I Healthy patient Lowest baseline risk
II Mild systemic disease Usually proceed
III Severe systemic disease Increased risk; optimize
IV Severe disease, constant threat to life Major perioperative risk
V Moribund, unlikely to survive without operation Surgery is lifesaving
VI Brain-dead organ donor Organ procurement
E Added for emergency Higher urgency/risk

  • Named sign/classification: Mallampati I–IV estimates potential airway difficulty but has limited sensitivity when used alone.

  • Study of choice: targeted testing based on comorbidity/procedure; routine tests are unnecessary in healthy patients having minor surgery.

  • Operate now: emergency disease even when ASA risk is high if delay threatens life.

  • Optimize/observe: elective procedure with reversible severe medical instability.

  • Procedure of choice for anticipated difficult airway: flexible fiberoptic intubation is described by Schwartz as a gold-standard technique.

  • Classic structure at risk: emergency cricothyrotomy → laryngeal structures; neuraxial anesthesia → neuraxis/epidural vessels.

  • Top complication: airway failure is immediate; malignant hyperthermia is intraoperative/early postoperative.

  • Contraindicated/wrong maneuver: repeated traumatic intubation attempts while oxygenation is failing; oxygenation comes first.

16. Patient Safety / Quality of Surgical Care

  • Named framework: WHO Safe Surgery checklist approach.

  • Never events: wrong patient/site/procedure, retained foreign body, and other clearly preventable serious events; Schwartz specifically lists wrong-site/patient/procedure and retained object.

  • Scoring: ASA contributes risk stratification; Schwartz also discusses a surgical “Apgar” concept based on intraoperative physiology, but the detailed point table was not retrieved from the source excerpt.

  • Operate now: only after patient, procedure, and site are verified.

  • Observe/stop: any unresolved discrepancy → STOP before incision.

  • Procedure of choice: not an operation; standardized verification, briefing, time-out, counts, handoff.

  • Classic structure at risk: entire patient through system error rather than one anatomy.

  • Top complication: wrong-site/retained-object events are immediate system failures; postoperative consequences vary.

  • Contraindicated: proceeding despite unresolved laterality, count, blood-product, specimen, or identity discrepancy.

  • Strong teamwork and allowing all OR personnel to “speak up” are central safety principles.

Contraindicated / Classic Wrong-Order Maneuvers

  • Unstable hemorrhagic trauma → CT first ❌

    • Correct: hemorrhage localization/control first.

  • Lactic acidosis from shock → bicarbonate instead of perfusion ❌

    • Correct: restore circulation/source control.

  • Threatened burn airway → wait for complete obstruction ❌

    • Correct: intubate early.

  • Circumferential eschar with vascular compromise → more fluid alone ❌

    • Correct: escharotomy when restriction is the problem.

  • True deep compartment syndrome → escharotomy only ❌

    • Correct: fasciotomy.

  • Abscess → antibiotics alone ❌

    • Correct: drainage/source control.

  • Necrotizing infection → delay debridement for extensive imaging ❌

    • Correct: immediate operative source control when clinically established.

  • Fascial dehiscence/evisceration → bedside reduction and dressing only ❌

    • Correct: protect viscera → resuscitate → urgent OR.

  • Massive hemorrhage → crystalloids indefinitely ❌

    • Correct: blood-product resuscitation + hemorrhage control.

  • Suspected transfusion reaction → continue blood while observing ❌

    • Correct: stop transfusion immediately.

  • Cancer → largest possible operation regardless of margins/morbidity ❌

    • Correct: oncologically adequate negative-margin resection.

  • Abrupt transplant dysfunction → assume rejection immediately ❌

    • Correct: exclude vascular/technical catastrophe first.

  • Difficult airway → repeated intubation attempts while SpO₂ falls ❌

    • Correct: prioritize oxygenation and rescue ventilation.

  • Wrong-site uncertainty → proceed while staff clarify ❌

    • Correct: stop until identity/site/procedure are resolved.

I. ESOPHAGUS

Diaphragmatic / Hiatal Hernia

Rapid-review item Board point
Named sign / classification Type I sliding; Type II paraesophageal; Type III mixed; Type IV additional organ in chest. Borchardt triad [SUPPLEMENTARY] = severe epigastric/chest pain + unproductive retching + inability to pass NG tube → suspect acute gastric volvulus.
Score / cutoff → decision No numerical source-defined score. Short esophagus: failure to obtain ~2.5 cm tension-free intra-abdominal esophagus after mobilization → add esophageal-lengthening procedure.
Imaging of choice Contrast esophagram for hernia anatomy/volvulus; EGD for mucosal disease, bleeding, Barrett change, or obstruction.
OPERATE NOW Volvulus, strangulation, ischemia, perforation, acute obstruction, uncontrolled bleeding.
OBSERVE Selected truly asymptomatic paraesophageal hernia; observation fails with progressive pain, vomiting, dysphagia, anemia, obstruction, or volvulus.
Procedure of choice Laparoscopic paraesophageal hernia repair + crural repair ± fundoplication; Collis gastroplasty if true short esophagus.
Classic structure at risk Vagal trunks → delayed gastric emptying/foregut dysfunction.
Top complication Recurrent hiatal hernia; exact POD not applicable because usually late.
Contraindicated / wrong move Do not treat an ischemic/strangulated intrathoracic stomach with prolonged observation.

Achalasia

Rapid-review item Board point
Named sign Bird-beak sign on contrast study → supports achalasia but does not exclude pseudoachalasia.
Score / cutoff → decision Eckardt score [SUPPLEMENTARY]: dysphagia + regurgitation + chest pain + weight loss, each 0–3; ≤3 = remission, >3 = persistent treatment failure.
Source physiologic cutoff Post-treatment LES pressure <10 mmHg → favorable response; >20 mmHg after dilation predicts poorer durable response.
Diagnostic test of choice High-resolution manometry confirms; EGD excludes mechanical obstruction/pseudoachalasia.
OPERATE / definitive therapy Symptomatic fit patient requiring durable treatment.
OBSERVE / temporize Botulinum/medical therapy mainly for poor procedural candidates.
Procedure of choice Laparoscopic Heller myotomy + partial fundoplication; POEM is an endoscopic alternative.
Classic structure at risk Esophageal mucosa → perforation/leak.
Top complication Mucosal leak, usually POD 1–5 [SUPPLEMENTARY]; later GERD is common.
Contraindicated / wrong move Fundoplication alone without relieving LES obstruction; overly tight complete wrap in severely impaired motility.

Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease

Rapid-review item Board point
Named classification Los Angeles A: breaks ≤5 mm; B: >5 mm but confined to folds; C: crosses folds but <75% circumference; D: ≥75%.
Score / cutoff → decision DeMeester >14.72 [SUPPLEMENTARY] = abnormal acid exposure; supports objective GERD but is not an operative indication by itself.
Imaging/test of choice EGD for anatomy/mucosa; prolonged ambulatory pH monitoring for objective acid exposure; manometry before surgery when motility must be defined.
OPERATE Objectively documented troublesome GERD despite appropriate medical treatment, medication intolerance, or reflux-related complications in a properly selected patient.
OBSERVE / medical Symptoms controlled medically without progressive complications.
Procedure of choice Laparoscopic fundoplication: Nissen 360° with normal motility; Toupet 270° commonly considered with impaired motility.
Classic structure at risk Vagus; spleen/short gastrics also vulnerable.
Top complication Postoperative dysphagia, immediate to early weeks [SUPPLEMENTARY].
Contraindicated / wrong move Antireflux surgery for uninvestigated dysphagia or symptoms without objective evidence when diagnosis is uncertain.

Caustic Esophageal Injury

Rapid-review item Board point
Named classification Source algorithm: 1° vs 2°/3° vs full-thickness necrosis. Zargar [SUPPLEMENTARY]: 0–3B; higher grade → greater perforation/stricture risk.
Score / cutoff → decision 1°/viable → observe; questionable deep injury → explore/second look; full-thickness necrosis → resection.
Diagnostic study of choice Schwartz algorithm: esophagoscopy within ~12 h.
OPERATE NOW Full-thickness necrosis, perforation, mediastinitis/peritonitis, uncontrolled hemorrhage, progressive shock.
OBSERVE Superficial viable injury → approximately 24–48 h observation.
DAMAGE CONTROL Necrotic unstable patient → remove nonviable esophagus/stomach, diversion + feeding access; resect other necrotic organs as required.
DEFINITIVE late repair Refractory stricture → esophageal replacement/reconstruction after acute phase.
Procedure of choice Esophagogastric resection for transmural necrosis; second-look ~36 h when viability remains questionable.
Classic structure at risk Recurrent laryngeal nerve/airway/thoracic duct during esophageal resection.
Top complication Leak/sepsis in first postoperative week, often POD 3–7 [SUPPLEMENTARY]; late stricture.
Contraindicated maneuver Induced emesis; Schwartz specifically warns it renews caustic exposure and may precipitate perforation.

Barrett Esophagus

Rapid-review item Board point
Named finding Salmon-colored distal columnar mucosa; diagnosis requires biopsy demonstrating intestinal metaplasia.
Classification → decision No dysplasia → surveillance; low-grade/indefinite → high-dose acid suppression then repeat biopsy; high-grade/intramucosal disease → endoscopic eradication; T1b+ → consider esophagectomy.
Imaging/test of choice EGD with systematic biopsy.
OPERATE / definitive resection Invasive T1b or deeper cancer, selected multifocal/long-segment disease unsuitable for endoscopic control.
OBSERVE / endoscopic pathway Nondysplastic Barrett and appropriately selected superficial neoplasia.
Procedure of choice EMR ± radiofrequency ablation for appropriate superficial disease; esophagectomy for invasive disease.
Classic structure at risk Recurrent laryngeal nerve during esophagectomy.
Top complication After esophagectomy: anastomotic leak, often POD 5–7 [SUPPLEMENTARY]; after ablation: delayed stricture.
Contraindicated / wrong move Stopping Barrett surveillance solely because fundoplication was performed.

Boerhaave Syndrome

Rapid-review item Board point
Named signs Mackler triad [SUPPLEMENTARY]: vomiting + chest pain + subcutaneous emphysema. Hamman sign [SUPPLEMENTARY]: mediastinal crunch. Both are insensitive when absent.
Scoring system No source-defined numerical operative score.
Imaging of choice Water-soluble contrast esophagram; CT defines mediastinal/pleural contamination.
OPERATE NOW Free thoracic leak, systemic sepsis, hemodynamic instability, extensive pleural/mediastinal contamination, necrotic tissue.
OBSERVE / NOM Carefully selected contained perforation draining back into esophagus, mild symptoms, minimal sepsis.
Failure of NOM Fever/sepsis, enlarging collection, respiratory deterioration, continued leak, instability.
DAMAGE CONTROL Destructive delayed perforation/unstable patient → drainage ± diversion/exclusion/resection.
DEFINITIVE REPAIR Primary repair + reinforcement + wide drainage when tissue is viable, particularly with early diagnosis.
Classic structure at risk Aorta, vagus, airway, thoracic duct.
Top complication Persistent repair leak/mediastinitis, usually first week, POD 3–7 [SUPPLEMENTARY].
Contraindicated / wrong move Repeated diagnostic testing that delays source control in a septic patient with established free perforation.

Mallory-Weiss Syndrome

Rapid-review item Board point
Named pattern Hematemesis after forceful retching; mucosal tear, not full-thickness rupture.
Score / cutoff Glasgow-Blatchford ≤1 → very-low-risk UGIB pathway; Rockall ≤2 → low rebleeding/death risk.
Imaging/test of choice EGD.
OPERATE NOW Rare: persistent life-threatening hemorrhage not controlled endoscopically/interventionally.
OBSERVE Bleeding has stopped and patient is hemodynamically low risk.
Procedure of choice Endoscopic hemostasis; surgery, when unavoidable, = direct oversewing via gastrotomy.
Classic structure at risk GEJ/vagal structures during operative exposure.
Top complication Rebleeding, usually first 24–72 h [SUPPLEMENTARY].
Contraindicated / wrong move Treating as Boerhaave; Schwartz also notes balloon tamponade is not reliable definitive control.

Esophageal Tumors

Rapid-review item Board point
Named benign lesion Leiomyoma = most common benign esophageal tumor in Schwartz; smooth intramural mass, usually distal two-thirds.
TNM cutoffs T1 mucosa/submucosa; T2 muscularis propria; T3 adventitia; T4a pleura/pericardium/diaphragm; T4b aorta/trachea/vertebra etc.; N1 1–2, N2 3–6, N3 ≥7; M1 distant disease.
Imaging/staging of choice EGD biopsy → EUS for T/N → CT ± PET for metastasis.
OPERATE / curative pathway Resectable localized invasive cancer after stage-appropriate therapy.
DO NOT CURATIVELY RESECT T4b unresectable critical-structure invasion or distant metastatic disease in routine setting.
Procedure of choice Appropriate esophagectomy; leiomyoma → enucleation.
Classic structure at risk Recurrent laryngeal nerve → hoarseness/aspiration; thoracic duct → chylothorax.
Top complication Pneumonia POD 1–5 [SUPPLEMENTARY] or anastomotic leak POD 5–7 [SUPPLEMENTARY].
Contraindicated / wrong move Biopsying a classic freely mobile leiomyoma through intact mucosa before planned enucleation; direct esophagectomy without staging.

II. STOMACH AND DUODENUM

Peptic Ulcer Disease — Perforation / Obstruction

Rapid-review item Board point
Named finding Sudden severe pain + board-like guarding/rebound → perforation; succussion splash [SUPPLEMENTARY] supports gastric outlet obstruction but is insensitive/nonspecific.

CONTRAINDICATED / CLASSIC WRONG MOVES

  • Caustic ingestion: do not induce vomiting.

  • Boerhaave + sepsis/free leak: do not pursue prolonged nonoperative observation.

  • Mallory-Weiss: do not confuse a mucosal tear with transmural rupture requiring thoracotomy.

  • Achalasia: do not perform fundoplication without myotomy.

  • GERD: do not operate on unexplained symptoms without adequate anatomic/physiologic evaluation.

  • Leiomyoma: do not routinely biopsy a classic intact intramural lesion through normal mucosa before enucleation.

  • Unstable perforated PUD: do not choose major gastric resection when abbreviated source control will save the patient.

  • Posterior duodenal bleed: do not forget the gastroduodenal artery.

  • Gastric GIST: do not perform routine lymphadenectomy.

  • Closed-loop/ischemic SBO: do not wait 48–72 hours for conservative management.

  • Ileus: do not operate solely for absent bowel sounds without evidence of a mechanical/septic cause.

  • Intestinal TB: do not resect uncomplicated disease that should be medically treated.

  • Retroperitoneal hematoma: do not open a contained vascular hematoma before establishing proximal control.

  • Evisceration: do not reduce/tape at bedside; cover bowel and return urgently to the OR.

I. BILIARY TRACT

Acute Cholangitis

Rapid-review item Board point
Named signs Charcot triad = fever + RUQ/epigastric pain + jaundice; present in only about two-thirds, so absence does not exclude disease. Reynolds pentad = Charcot triad + shock + altered mental status → severe septic cholangitis.
Scoring / cutoff No numeric cholangitis score was reproduced in the retrieved source. Hemodynamic instability, organ failure, altered mentation, and persistent sepsis determine urgency.
Imaging of choice Ultrasound first for stones/ductal dilatation; CT/MRI for mass or alternate cause. Imaging should not delay drainage in a septic patient.
INTERVENE NOW Sepsis with biliary obstruction, Reynolds pentad, worsening organ dysfunction, persistent obstruction despite antibiotics/resuscitation.
Procedure of choice ERCP with sphincterotomy ± stone extraction/stent/drainage.
Alternative if ERCP fails PTC, EUS-guided drainage, or surgical drainage according to anatomy/expertise.
Definitive repair Gallstone-related cholangitis → interval cholecystectomy after resolution; Schwartz cites approximately 6 weeks.
Classic structure at risk Pancreatic duct/ampulla during ERCP → pancreatitis.
Top complication Post-ERCP pancreatitis, usually within 6–24 h [SUPPLEMENTARY].
Contraindicated / wrong move Cholecystostomy as primary treatment of cholangitis; source states infection is in the obstructed biliary tree, not the gallbladder.

Gallstones / Acute Cholecystitis

Rapid-review item Board point
Named sign Murphy sign / sonographic Murphy sign supports acute cholecystitis; absence does not exclude it, especially in older or diabetic patients.
Scoring / cutoff No source-specific numeric cholecystitis score retrieved.
Imaging of choice Ultrasound first; stones, wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid, sonographic Murphy. If equivocal → HIDA.
Key cutoff HIDA nonvisualization after 4 h → obstructed cystic duct and strongly supports acute cholecystitis; normal gallbladder filling argues against it.
OPERATE Acute cholecystitis in a fit patient → early surgery; gangrene, perforation, or uncontrolled sepsis makes source control urgent.
Procedure of choice Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy, preferably within ~72 h from onset in the source.
OBSERVE / temporize Critically ill/unfit → IV antibiotics + percutaneous cholecystostomy. About 90% improve in the cited source.
Failure of temporizing treatment Persistent sepsis, gangrene, perforation, clinical deterioration.
Classic structure at risk Common bile duct/common hepatic duct.
Preventive maneuver Critical view of safety: clear hepatocystic triangle, expose cystic plate, see exactly two structures entering gallbladder.
Top complication Bile leak, typically POD 1–7 [SUPPLEMENTARY].
Contraindicated / wrong move Blind clipping when anatomy is unclear; use bailout/conversion rather than guessing.

Choledocholithiasis

Rapid-review item Board point
Named clue Intermittent biliary pain + jaundice; transient obstruction may behave like a “ball-valve” stone.
Cutoff CBD >8 mm on ultrasound in a patient with gallstones, jaundice, and biliary pain is highly suggestive of CBD stones.
Imaging of choice Ultrasound first; uncertain duct stone → MRCP or EUS; MRCP sensitivity ~95%, specificity ~89% in source.
INTERVENE NOW Choledocholithiasis with cholangitis, persistent obstruction, or gallstone pancreatitis with cholangitis.
Procedure of choice ERCP with stone extraction, followed by cholecystectomy, OR one-stage laparoscopic cholecystectomy + IOC/CBD exploration.
OBSERVE No prolonged observation if persistent jaundice, cholangitis, or pancreatitis develops.
Classic structure at risk CBD/duodenum/pancreatic ampulla depending intervention.
Top complication Post-ERCP pancreatitis, 6–24 h [SUPPLEMENTARY].
Contraindicated / wrong move Purely diagnostic ERCP when MRCP/EUS can answer the question and no therapeutic intervention is planned.

Obstructive Jaundice

Rapid-review item Board point
Named sign Courvoisier sign [SUPPLEMENTARY] = palpable nontender gallbladder + jaundice → suggests malignant distal obstruction; absence is insensitive.
Laboratory pattern Conjugated hyperbilirubinemia + alkaline phosphatase elevation = cholestatic pattern.
Imaging of choice Ultrasound first. Duct dilation + stones → ERCP pathway; duct dilation without stones → contrast CT for malignancy.
INTERVENE NOW Obstructive jaundice + cholangitis/sepsis → urgent biliary drainage before full oncologic staging.
DEFINITIVE repair Distal resectable malignant obstruction → Whipple; selected hilar cholangiocarcinoma → bile-duct resection ± hepatectomy according to extent.
OBSERVE / palliate Unresectable/metastatic malignant obstruction → endoscopic metal stent or percutaneous drainage, not radical operation.
Classic structure at risk Portal vein/hepatic artery/biliary radicals in hilar surgery.

CONTRAINDICATED / CLASSIC WRONG MANEUVERS

  • Obstructive jaundice: do not delay biliary drainage in a septic patient for complete cancer staging.

  • Cirrhosis: do not perform elective nontransplant surgery routinely in severe decompensation or MELD >15 without compelling indication.

  • Variceal bleeding: do not use balloon tamponade as definitive treatment or for >36 hours.

  • Splenic trauma: do not perform automatic splenectomy in a stable patient solely because CT shows a high-grade lesion.

  • Post-splenectomy fever: do not delay antibiotics while waiting for culture results when OPSI is suspected.

1. Skin Grafting

Board point Rapid review
Named sign / key finding No formal named sign; graft-bed suitability = clean, vascularized, granulating surface with reliable hemostasis
Classification STSG = epidermis + part of dermis; FTSG = epidermis + entire dermis
Cutoff / decision Graft take: plasmatic imbibition 0–48 h → inosculation after ~48 h → revascularization by day 4–5
